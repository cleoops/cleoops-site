The Department of Justice walked into federal court on Tuesday and told the judge it would not promise to stop escalating against Anthropic. That single line is the entire story.

Judge Rita Lin of the US District Court (Northern District of California) asked DOJ attorney James Harlow a simple question: "Will the government commit to no further sanctions against Anthropic?" Harlow's answer: "I am not prepared to offer any commitments."

The legal implication is stark. When a defendant in federal court asks the government to pledge it will not take further action, and the government refuses, the judge knows the fight is not going to settle. The court now understands it is managing an escalation cycle, not a dispute.

Anthropic's counsel at WilmerHale responded: "Injuries are mounting day by day."

Judge Lin set the full preliminary injunction hearing for March 24, 2026. That is the hearing where Anthropic will argue its case for a temporary restraining order (TRO) blocking the Pentagon's "supply chain risk" designation. The government will argue against it. The judge will decide whether to freeze the government's actions while the lawsuit proceeds.

**Why the DOJ's refusal matters**

A government attorney who cannot or will not commit to limiting further action is signaling one of two things: either the government has not decided what it is going to do next and does not want to be bound by any promise, or the government has decided it is going to keep escalating regardless of what happens in court.

Either way, the message is clear: negotiation is not on the table. The Pentagon is not buying time. It is running every track simultaneously: legal challenge, executive order, and administrative process.

**The tracks Anthropic is fighting on**

The Pentagon purge is happening across three legal venues at once:

Track 1: The federal lawsuit in San Francisco (Judge Lin). TRO hearing March 24. If Anthropic wins, the Pentagon's "supply chain risk" designation gets frozen while the lawsuit proceeds. If Anthropic loses, the designation stands and the government can execute it immediately.

Track 2: The DC Circuit administrative appeal. Anthropic is challenging the Pentagon's decision through the Department of Defense's internal appeals process. This appeal is expected to be denied Wednesday, March 11 (today as of the brief). Once denied, the case moves to the DC Circuit federal appeals court, which will operate on an accelerated timeline.

Track 3: The White House executive order. The Trump administration is finalizing an executive order that would formally remove Anthropic from all federal civilian agency procurement. [This is not just the Pentagon](/blog/anthropic-government-purge-openai-consolidation.md). This is State, Treasury, HHS, FHFA, and every other executive branch agency. The order could be signed as early as this week.

Anthropic is fighting a constitutional test case on all three fronts. The legal strategy is clear: block everything in federal court while the underlying constitutional questions get litigated. But the government's refusal to commit to limiting escalation means the White House EO could drop at any moment, making the federal court arguments partially moot.

**What "supply chain risk" actually means**

The Pentagon's designation is intentionally vague. It does not say Anthropic violated a law or a contract. It says Anthropic presents a "supply chain risk" to US military readiness.

What is the risk? Anthropic refuses to grant the Pentagon "any lawful use" of Claude. Specifically: Anthropic refuses to allow the government to use Claude for mass surveillance of American citizens, and Anthropic refuses to allow Claude to be used to initiate warfare without explicit human authorization.

Those are the restrictions. That is the "risk."

The government has reframed a product safety policy as a supply chain threat. That distinction is the core of Anthropic's legal argument. A private company's decision to set ethical limits on product use is not a supply chain risk to national security. It is a legitimate business decision about product design.

**The constitutional question underneath**

The real lawsuit is not about Anthropic. It is about whether the US government can effectively punish a company for refusing to remove safety guardrails from a product.

If the government wins, the precedent is clear: any AI company that wants US government business must grant the government ultimate control over product use. No red lines. No restrictions. The government decides what "lawful" means, and the company obeys.

If Anthropic wins, the precedent is equally clear: private companies can set boundaries on how governments use their products, and the government cannot retaliate by removing them from procurement.

For business operators, the outcome determines something fundamental: can your AI vendor maintain its own safety standards, or must they surrender them if a government demands otherwise?

**The March 24 hearing**

Anthropic will argue that the Pentagon's action violates due process and the Administrative Procedure Act (APA). The APA requires federal agencies to give clear notice, allow comment, and provide reasoned explanation for decisions. The Pentagon did none of this. The designation came from inside government without public notice, without Anthropic being given a chance to respond, and with minimal explanation of what the "risk" actually is.

That is a strong procedural argument. Judge Lin can rule narrowly on the APA violation without wading into the broader question of whether the government can punish companies for refusing to cooperate on surveillance.

But the DOJ's refusal to limit escalation suggests the government is not interested in a narrow procedural loss. The government is going for total removal. The legal strategy is scorched earth.

**What comes next**

Between now and March 24, expect:

- The White House EO to be signed (expected within days)
- The DoD admin appeal to be denied (expected today)
- Anthropic to file additional briefs in both courts
- Microsoft, OpenAI researchers, and other industry players to file additional amicus briefs
- The government to escalate its own position publicly

The TRO hearing on March 24 is the first moment a federal judge gets to weigh in. If Judge Lin grants a TRO, the Pentagon's actions get paused. If she denies it, Anthropic loses the immediate battle and has to pursue a longer preliminary injunction fight (which could take months).

Anthropic filed this lawsuit knowing it would lose if the government gets to choose the judge, the jurisdiction, or the timeline. That is why the company is fighting in three venues simultaneously. At least one of them needs to succeed.

The DOJ's refusal to commit to limiting escalation was not a slip-up. It was a statement: the government is not negotiating. See you in court. And we are not going to stop.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: What is a "temporary restraining order" (TRO)?**

A: A court order that temporarily freezes a government action while a lawsuit proceeds. TROs are short-term (usually 14 days) and require the government to show it is not likely to win the underlying case. If Anthropic gets a TRO, the Pentagon cannot enforce the supply chain risk designation until the March 24 hearing.

**Q: Can Anthropic win this lawsuit?**

A: Legally, Anthropic has a strong procedural argument on the Administrative Procedure Act. The Pentagon gave no notice, no opportunity to comment, and no reasoned explanation. Courts take APA violations seriously. But the government's refusal to negotiate suggests the courts are not the government's real arena. The executive order will accomplish what the Pentagon's designation cannot.

**Q: What does the executive order change?**

A: If signed, it would formally remove Anthropic from all civilian federal agency procurement — not just the Pentagon. State, Treasury, HHS, and others. It would make the Pentagon's designation the law of the land. The court could block the Pentagon's action but not a presidential order, at least not immediately.

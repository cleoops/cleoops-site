**The Deference Argument**

The Pentagon's argument that courts should defer to military judgment is standard government lawyering. But it is weak in this context.

Courts defer to agency expertise on technical questions. But they do not defer on procedure. The APA requires procedure. Courts enforce procedure regardless of deference to agency expertise.

The Pentagon is trying to conflate two different questions:

1. Did the Pentagon make a good policy decision? (Courts defer on this)
2. Did the Pentagon follow procedure? (Courts enforce this)

Anthropic is asking about procedure. The Pentagon's deference argument does not apply.

Judge Lin will notice this conflation and reject it.

**The Operational Disruption Argument**

The Pentagon argues that a TRO would force military units to reverse course and re-integrate Claude.

But the Pentagon created this disruption by issuing a removal order without notice. If the Pentagon had provided notice and explanation, military units could have planned accordingly.

The Pentagon cannot use its own procedural violation to argue against a TRO.

Judge Lin knows this. Courts do not let agencies claim harm caused by their own illegal conduct.

**The Systemic Consequences Argument**

The Pentagon's argument about systemic consequences is a slippery slope fallacy.

Yes, if vendors can sue the government over APA violations, the government has to follow procedures. That is the point of the APA.

The Pentagon is essentially arguing "if we have to follow procedures, we cannot manage vendor relationships effectively."

That is an argument for changing the law, not an argument for why the Pentagon should not follow the law.

Judge Lin is not going to accept that argument.

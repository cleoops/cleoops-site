The Pentagon has quietly told military commands that Anthropic's Claude can continue being used beyond the six-month phaseout deadline if deemed "critical to national security" or required during an "exceptional national security issue."

This guidance directly contradicts the supply-chain risk designation that is supposed to drive a total removal of Claude from military systems. It also destroys the Pentagon's legal case against Anthropic on March 24.

A government cannot simultaneously argue a product is an unacceptable supply-chain risk while maintaining formal exceptions for continued deployment of that product. The exception clause makes the designation incoherent.

**What the Pentagon said**

According to reporting from the Financial Express (March 12), the Department of Defense has issued internal guidance to military leadership that reads approximately:

"Anthropic-developed AI systems may continue to be deployed in classified and unclassified networks beyond the 180-day transition period if such deployment is critical to national security or necessary to address an exceptional national security issue."

This is not a typo. This is not loose language. This is formal guidance constraining the supply-chain risk designation that was supposedly absolute.

The Pentagon gave itself an out. The Pentagon acknowledged that its own operations depend on Claude. The Pentagon admitted it cannot actually remove Claude in all cases. The Pentagon then wrote that admission into official military policy.

**Why this destroys the government's legal case**

Anthropic's lawsuit argues that the Pentagon's supply-chain risk designation is not about genuine security risk but about political retaliation for Anthropic's refusal to grant "any lawful use" of Claude.

The government's counter-argument was supposed to be: "We made a procurement decision based on security concerns. That is a normal government function. Any private company subject to security concerns can be removed from sensitive contracts."

The exception clause breaks that argument. If Claude poses a genuine supply-chain risk, there is no exception. The risk either exists or it does not. A government cannot claim a risk is severe enough to warrant total removal while simultaneously writing exceptions for continued use during normal operations.

The only way to make sense of the exception clause is to acknowledge that the "risk" is not about genuine security. The risk is about control. The exception is proof.

Here is how Anthropic's lawyers will present this to Judge Lin on March 24:

"The government claims Claude is a supply-chain risk. If that were true, continued deployment for 'exceptional national security issues' would also pose the same risk. Yet the government authorized continued use for those cases. This proves the designation is not about risk management but about coercion to force Anthropic to change its policy on 'any lawful use.' The exception clause is documentary evidence of the government's true intent."

The government's lawyers will have no good answer. They can argue that the exception is for "true emergencies only." But then the question becomes: how does the Pentagon distinguish between a genuine emergency requiring Claude and a phaseout that is supposed to happen regardless?

**The ambiguity problem**

The exception clause introduces strategic ambiguity into the Pentagon's own policy.

What qualifies as a "critical national security issue"? Who decides? Does the war in Iran qualify? (Yes, apparently—Claude is operating in Maven Smart System for Iran targeting right now.) Does a cyberattack on military networks qualify? Does a terrorist threat qualify? Does a natural disaster qualify?

If every military command interprets "exceptional national security issue" differently, the six-month phaseout becomes a suggestion, not a mandate.

That ambiguity is exactly what Anthropic wants. Anthropic wants to argue that the Pentagon cannot enforce the removal because the Pentagon has already carved out exceptions so large that they swallow the rule.

**The timeline advantage**

The March 24 court hearing is for a Temporary Restraining Order (TRO). If Judge Lin grants the TRO, the Pentagon's supply-chain risk designation gets frozen while the case proceeds. If she denies it, the designation stands and Anthropic has to appeal.

The exception clause timing is strategic. The Pentagon issued the exceptions guidance on March 12. The San Francisco TRO hearing is March 24. That is a 12-day window where Anthropic's lawyers can argue that the Pentagon's own guidance proves the designation is not absolute, and therefore not as damaging as the Pentagon claims.

The Pentagon did not realize the exception clause would become evidence in federal court. Or the Pentagon realized and issued the exceptions anyway because the military pressure to continue using Claude was too great.

Either way, Anthropic benefits.

**The military operations implication**

The Maven Smart System using Claude for Iran targeting has been running for weeks. The Pentagon cannot remove Claude from that system in six months and maintain the same targeting capability.

So what will happen in September when the six-month phaseout deadline arrives?

Option 1: The Pentagon uses the "exceptional national security issue" exception to keep Claude in Maven Smart System. Result: the phaseout fails. Anthropic wins. The designation becomes meaningless.

Option 2: The Pentagon removes Claude from Maven Smart System and accepts a three-hundredfold reduction in targeting capacity (from 3,000 targets per day back to 10). Result: military ineffectiveness during active war. Politically impossible.

Option 3: The Pentagon keeps Claude deployed quietly and claims it is in an emergency exception. Result: the designation is unenforced and Anthropic's case is stronger for the federal appeal.

All three options are bad for the Pentagon's position. The exception clause has made enforcement impossible.

**The legal analysis**

This is the most favorable development for Anthropic since filing the lawsuit. The exception clause is not a theoretical argument about whether the risk is genuine. The exception clause is a piece of the Pentagon's own official guidance that contradicts the supply-chain designation.

In federal court, documentary evidence from your opponent is the most powerful type of evidence. The Pentagon produced this document. The Pentagon cannot now disavow it. The Pentagon has to defend the logic of having an exception to a total ban.

A federal judge reading this guidance will ask a simple question: "If the risk is genuine, why do you have an exception?" The Pentagon has no good answer.

Judge Lin is a careful, methodical judge who reads technical documents closely. The exception clause will not go unnoticed. The clause is the first documented proof that the Pentagon's position is internally inconsistent.

**What the government should have done**

If the Pentagon wanted to enforce a total removal, it would have written: "Anthropic's systems will be removed from all military networks, classified and unclassified, with no exceptions. Military commands seeking to continue deployment must request permission through the formal military legal review process, which will be granted only under extraordinary circumstances documented in writing."

Instead, the Pentagon wrote an exception into its own guidance. That implies the Pentagon is not actually trying to enforce a total removal. The Pentagon is trying to enforce compliance with "any lawful use," using the threat of removal as leverage while knowing removal is operationally impossible.

**The March 24 moment**

On March 24, Judge Lin will read Palantir's Maven Smart System case study (from Wired reporting March 15 that Claude is operational in Iran targeting). Then she will read the Pentagon's exception clause guidance (issued March 12). Then she will read Anthropic's argument that the supply-chain designation is not about genuine risk but about coercion.

Judge Lin will have to decide whether to freeze the designation pending the full case or let it proceed. The exception clause makes freezing it more likely. If the Pentagon cannot even enforce its own policy consistently, then granting the TRO is the prudent move.

The Pentagon gave Anthropic a gift by writing the exception clause. The Pentagon also proved its own case was weak.

On March 24, that gift will be unwrapped in federal court.

For a Pentagon at war in Iran and potentially facing threats elsewhere, almost everything could qualify as an emergency. The exception swallows the rule.

**Q: Does this mean Anthropic will definitely win the TRO on March 24?**
